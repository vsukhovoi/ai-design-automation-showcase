# AI Visual Storytelling & Design Automation | Fun Cruise Stories

**AI Artist portfolio · Character consistency · Visual direction · Prompt engineering · Production workflow design**

This portfolio presents a selection of AI-assisted illustrations and the visual production approach developed for **Fun Cruise Stories**, an episodic illustrated storytelling project for an American audience aged 55+. The series is planned around a 120-episode narrative; this number describes the creative scope, **not a claim that 120 episodes have been completed**.

My focus is on translating scripts and creative briefs into coherent scenes: maintaining recognizable recurring characters, selecting references, directing composition and lighting, and reviewing generated results. The gallery below shows 15 illustrative examples.

**Watch the project:** [Fun Cruise Stories on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@FunCruiseStories)

## What I contribute

- **Art direction:** scene interpretation, visual style, mood, composition, and lighting decisions.
- **Character continuity:** reference sheets and repeatable descriptions for faces, age, accessories, silhouettes, and costumes.
- **Prompt engineering:** structured prompts combining persistent character attributes with scene-specific actions and environments.
- **Visual quality review:** human evaluation of anatomy, hands, props, scene continuity, and adherence to the brief; revision where necessary.
- **Workflow design:** documentation and exploration of repeatable production steps and opportunities for automation.

## Character Consistency: Visual DNA

Recurring characters need to remain recognizable across settings, costumes, lighting conditions, and story timelines. The approach separates **stable identity cues** from **changeable scene attributes** rather than assuming that a generative model can guarantee a perfect character lock.

**Example — Loretta Calhoun (2012)**

- **Identity cues:** age (72), facial proportions, distinctive rhinestone cat-eye glasses, and overall silhouette.
- **Scene-dependent cues:** wardrobe, pose, emotion, environment, and period-specific styling.
- **Review criteria:** resemblance to reference images, anatomical plausibility, costume continuity, and narrative fit.

A proposed planning matrix covers **12 characters × 15 costume contexts = 180 combinations**. This is a production-planning structure, not evidence that 180 finished reference sheets have been generated.

## Creative Process & Making-of

1. **Interpret the brief:** identify characters, location, time period, narrative action, format, and visual priorities.
2. **Gather references:** use character sheets and scene references to establish visual continuity.
3. **Build the prompt:** specify persistent identity traits, composition, lighting, wardrobe, props, and exclusions.
4. **Generate and evaluate:** review results for resemblance, object interaction, anatomy, style, and framing.
5. **Revise and select:** adjust prompts or references and choose the most suitable final image.
6. **Prepare deliverables:** adapt framing for 16:9 episodes or 9:16 short-form content as required.

**Breakdown status:** the gallery currently emphasizes finished artwork. To make the process independently reviewable, a future case study should include a source brief, reference images (with appropriate permissions), prompt excerpts, intermediate iterations, and a final selected frame. These materials are **not presented as already included** in this repository.

### Workflow concept

```text
Script / creative brief
         ↓
Character and scene references
         ↓
Structured prompt and image generation
         ↓
Human review and iteration
         ↓
Selected artwork and format-specific assets
```

Python/API and CLI automation can support stages of this process; the diagram is a **workflow overview**, not a claim of fully unattended generation.

## Practical Value

The approach is designed to make recurring visual production easier to organize and review by reusing character specifications, standardizing briefs, and separating creative decisions from repetitive preparation tasks.

**No quantitative productivity or cost-saving claim is made here.** Reliable figures would require documented baseline timings, comparable workloads, and repeatable measurements.
